# 6. Getting Ranked

*Being indexed just means you're in the pool. Ranking is about where you land in it, and that's decided fresh for every single search query.*

## What This Chapter Covers

This chapter covers what "ranking" actually means, and the main factors that separate a page that's indexed-but-buried from one that shows up near the top: search intent, relevance, content quality, internal linking, page experience, and trust/authority.

## Why It Matters

This is the step people expect to happen automatically once indexing is sorted out, and it's the one with the least direct control. There's no setting that ranks you higher. Ranking is Google's answer to "which of the pages I know about best serves this specific query, right now, compared to everything else that also claims to answer it." Improving it means improving how well your page actually serves searchers, not finding a technical trick.

## Key Concepts

- **Search intent** — what the searcher is actually trying to do: find information, navigate to a known site, compare options, or complete a transaction. A technically excellent page that answers the wrong intent (e.g. a product page for a query that wants a how-to guide) won't rank well no matter how optimized it is.
- **Relevance** — how directly the page's content addresses the query, including related terms and context, not just exact keyword matches.
- **Content quality** — depth, accuracy, and actual usefulness. A page that genuinely answers the question in more useful detail tends to outperform one that's thinner or padded.
- **Internal linking** — links between your own pages distribute both crawl paths and contextual signal about what a page is about and how important it is relative to the rest of the site.
- **Page experience** — Core Web Vitals (load speed, interactivity, visual stability), mobile usability, and the absence of intrusive interstitials. These act more as a tiebreaker than a primary factor, but a genuinely slow or broken experience can hold a page back.
- **Trust and authority** — signals like quality backlinks, consistent publishing, and content that demonstrates real experience or expertise on the topic. Often summarized as E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authority, trust) — not a literal score, but a useful lens for the kind of signals Google's systems try to approximate.

## Technical SEO vs. Content SEO

Technical SEO (Chapters 3–5) makes a page *eligible* to rank at all — it gets crawled, indexed, and correctly understood. Content and authority (this chapter) determine *how high* it ranks once it's in that pool. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient on its own. A technically flawless page with thin content won't outrank a well-written competitor, and brilliant content on a page blocked by `robots.txt` never gets the chance to compete.

## Why Indexing Doesn't Guarantee High Ranking

Indexing answers "is this page a candidate?" Ranking answers "is this page the *best* candidate, out of every other indexed page that could also answer this query?" For any competitive search term, that's a comparison against pages that have had months or years to build relevance and authority. A brand-new page can be perfectly indexed and still rank on page five simply because more established, more relevant pages already occupy the top results — not because anything is broken.

## Practical Tips

- Write for the person searching, then check the query still makes technical sense (title tag, headings) — not the other way around.
- Look at what's currently ranking for a target query before writing; it tells you the intent Google has already decided that query means.
- Internal links from your strongest pages to newer ones help those newer pages both get discovered and inherit some contextual relevance.
